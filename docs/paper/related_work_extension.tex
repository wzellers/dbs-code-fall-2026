\subsection*{Bandit formulations of DBS parameter search}

The closest prior treatment of DBS as a bandit problem comes from work on
stimulation for cognitive control. Nagrale and Widge~\cite{nagrale2026arena}
cast each electrode contact as an arm and compare UCB1, a variance-aware
UCB-Tuned, Block-Variance Bayes-UCB and several Thompson Sampling variants
against greedy, $\varepsilon$-greedy and brute-force search, selecting over
eight contacts using task reaction time as the reward. Their continuous
rolling arena keeps four contacts active, benches the weakest and rolls an
untested contact into the set, which they report matches full eight-contact
search at lower cost.

That scheme and our pruning share a premise --- that most of an arm set can be
set aside early --- and differ in what follows from it. The rolling arena never
discards permanently; it rotates, so every contact remains reachable and the
search runs continuously over a horizon of up to 4{,}200 trials. T3P prunes
once, to the top $K$, and recovers the discarded arms not by rotation but by
re-triggering the whole schedule on a timer or on a biomarker excursion, which
is what lets it converge in roughly two minutes rather than thousands of
trials. The arm sets differ accordingly: theirs is eight discrete contacts,
ours a two-dimensional frequency--amplitude grid.

The objectives differ more than the algorithms. Their reward is behavioural
reaction time on an interference task, a proxy for cognitive control, and
carries no energy term; ours is a biomarker-and-energy composite intended for
an implant. Contact selection and parameter tuning are also complementary
rather than competing problems --- one chooses where to stimulate, the other
how --- and~\cite{nagrale2026arena} names joint optimisation of amplitude,
pulse width and frequency as future work, which is the gap our joint
frequency--amplitude formulation addresses.

This line is also a useful case of a method correcting itself. The earlier
study from the same group~\cite{nagrale2023bayesian} fed the bandit a latent
state inferred by a sensor model and reset the search daily. The later paper
removes the sensor model and feeds raw reaction times directly, reporting that
the sensor model had inflated variance and raised ranking failure rates from
11.9\% to 31.9\%, and replaces daily resets with one continuous history. The
lesson transfers to our setting: an estimator placed between the measurement
and the learner can cost more in added variance than it recovers in
denoising.


\subsection*{Reducing the cost of learned controllers}

The deep-RL line has been extended less by new algorithms than by attempts to
make existing ones affordable. Mehregan et al.~\cite{mehregan2024enhancing}
discretise the state and action spaces of an RL controller and apply
post-training and quantisation-aware training, reporting that quantisation
perturbs individual policy outputs without degrading overall efficacy, and
validating in a rodent model as well as in simulation --- the only in-vivo
evidence among the works discussed here. Ravivarapu et
al.~\cite{ravivarapu2025sea} build on it directly, adopting the same group's
network model but targeting sample efficiency rather than model size: a
predictive reward model lets the policy update without querying the
environment every step, with Gumbel-Softmax sampling for exploration and FP16
quantisation from 65\,MB to 33\,MB.

The two differ in which cost they attack --- one compresses a trained policy,
the other reduces the interaction needed to train it --- and both differ from
our work in what is actually being decided. Each chooses at every step whether
to deliver a pulse, leaving frequency and amplitude fixed. Neither carries an
explicit energy term: SEA-DBS rewards the squared deviation of beta power from
a threshold, with stimulation cost entering only implicitly through sparsity,
where ours carries an RMS penalty and a no-stimulation bonus directly in the
objective. The deployment gap follows from the representation: a 33\,MB
quantised network remains orders of magnitude beyond the microcontrollers we
target, and neither paper reports measured energy on hardware. Both also
require offline training before first use, which is the cost our
warm-up-and-prune schedule removes rather than reduces.


\subsection*{Moving the computation rather than shrinking it}

Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2026neuromorphic} take the opposite route to
quantisation, changing the substrate instead of compressing the network: a
deep spiking Q-network with actuator energy in the reward, compressed by
sparsity-constrained knowledge distillation and deployed on a neuromorphic
processor. They report 45.2\% suppression of alpha--beta oscillations, an
80.0\% reduction in stimulation charge against continuous DBS, and 0.52\,mW
inference power.

This is the nearest work to ours in kind of evidence rather than in method. It
is the only other study here to place a learned DBS policy on real silicon and
measure what it costs, and like ours it puts energy in the objective rather
than treating it as a downstream consequence. The methods stay far apart: a
spiking Q-network is trained offline and then distilled, so the pipeline
producing the deployed policy is heavier than the policy itself, whereas a
pruned bandit is trained in place. The comparison a reviewer will want is
therefore total cost of obtaining a controller, including training, rather
than suppression against suppression.

\subsection*{Where the biology sits}

BGRL~\cite{bgrl2024} differs from the works above architecturally rather than
algorithmically. They use a biophysical basal-ganglia--thalamic circuit as the
\emph{environment} and pair it with a generic learner; BGRL draws on
basal-ganglia circuitry to structure the \emph{agent}. The two choices are
orthogonal and could in principle be combined.

\subsection*{Learning to tune a controller rather than the stimulus}

A separate thread keeps a classical controller and uses learning only to set
its parameters. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025pidnn} optimise a PID neural
network for the basal-ganglia network, pre-training the stimulation waveform
by system identification so no run-time tuning is needed, and derive a
Lyapunov sufficient condition for closed-loop stability within the identified
model; they report an $(81.0\pm0.7)\%$ reduction in control energy and
restored thalamic relay reliability. Mai et al.~\cite{mai2026personalized}
combine an unknown-input observer with a bounded soft actor--critic policy
that adjusts two observer and two feedback gains every 20\,ms, evaluated on a
delayed STN--GPe firing-rate model against conventional active disturbance
rejection control and sliding mode control, with a mean RMSE of 0.4143 against
0.4816 and 1.5000 spikes/s respectively.

Both tune a controller rather than the stimulus, which is the cleanest line
between this thread and ours: their action spaces are controller gains and
waveform parameters, not frequency and amplitude, so what is learned is how to
regulate rather than what to deliver. Two contrasts are worth conceding in the
text rather than leaving to a reviewer. First, neither our work nor any
RL-based study above offers a stability guarantee, and for a control audience
the Lyapunov condition in~\cite{zhang2025pidnn} is the clearest advantage of
the classical route. Second, the reported outcomes are not commensurable with
ours: thalamic relay reliability and firing-rate RMSE are different quantities
from band power, so the energy percentages are reductions against different
baselines over different variables. The shared motivation is nonetheless
ours --- pre-computing a policy so the device need not search at run time is
what warm-starting a bandit achieves, approached from the opposite direction.

\subsection*{What ``personalised'' is doing in this literature}

\cite{mai2026personalized} is worth a second remark, because its framing bears
on our own. It is titled as personalised adaptive suppression, but the
personalisation reported is robustness: the policy is trained under randomised
coupling and disturbance multipliers, and no patient data, severity grading or
per-individual calibration appears. The resulting controller is a single
policy intended to hold across conditions --- a generalist --- rather than one
selected or specialised per patient.

That is the same distinction our severity-selection argument turns on, and it
is usually left implicit. A policy robust to variation across patients and a
policy chosen for the patient in front of it are different objects with
different failure modes, and a controller can be called personalised for
achieving either. Stating which one a method delivers is a small clarification
that most of this literature, ours included, has not made explicit.


\subsection*{Constrained search and shared benchmarks}

Two further gaps are worth closing with citations. SAFE-OPT~\cite{safeopt2024}
adds explicit safety constraints to Bayesian optimisation of stimulation
parameters and is the nearest prior treatment of constrained parameter search
for DBS; it belongs wherever our own hard constraint is introduced. And two
works provide environments rather than controllers: Krylov et
al.~\cite{krylov2020framework} released an RL gym over ensembles of abstract
oscillators, with desynchronisation rather than band suppression as the
objective, and a more recent environment~\cite{benchmark2025env} targets the
same role with a neurophysiologically realistic model. The latter is the
natural venue for the cross-method comparison this section shows to be
missing: every work here improves on a baseline of its own choosing on its own
model, which is why none of the percentages quoted above can be ranked against
one another.
