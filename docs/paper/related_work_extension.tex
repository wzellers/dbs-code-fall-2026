\subsection*{Deciding when to stimulate}

Two recent works leave frequency and amplitude fixed and learn only whether to
deliver stimulation at each step. Mehregan et al.~\cite{mehregan2024enhancing}
discretise the state and action spaces of a DDPG-based controller. Their
reward is a function of average beta-band power alone, positive below a
threshold $\beta_t = 0.35$ and negative above it, with no stimulation term;
they report the trained controller settling to an average stimulation
frequency of 45\,Hz. They apply post-training quantisation and
quantisation-aware training to the model, and report results in a
computational model of Parkinson's disease and in a rat with Parkinson's
disease.

Ravivarapu et al.~\cite{ravivarapu2025sea} adopt the network model
of~\cite{mehregan2024enhancing}, and a reward of the same piecewise form about
the same threshold of 0.35, squared on both branches. They add two components:
a predictive reward model, which estimates expected rewards so the policy need
not rely on real-time feedback at every update, and Gumbel-Softmax sampling
for exploration in the binary action space. Their action is
$a_t \in \{0, 1\}$, where $1$ delivers a pulse. The reward carries no separate
stimulation term; the authors state that this form allows the agent to learn
temporally sparse stimulation patterns. They report robustness to FP16
post-training quantisation and a reduced memory footprint, without giving
model sizes, and compare against DDPG.


\subsection*{Deciding the stimulation parameters}

Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2026neuromorphic} tune the stimulus rather than its
timing. A deep spiking Q-network reads a biophysical cortico-basal
ganglia-thalamic model and emits nine discrete actions, one of
$\{-1, 0, +1\}$ for each of frequency, pulse width and amplitude, so each step
increases, decreases or maintains a parameter rather than selecting a value.
Pulse width is constrained to 60--400\,\textmu s and stimulation is
current-controlled. The reward combines alpha--beta suppression with the
physical charge delivered. The policy is compressed by sparsity-constrained
knowledge distillation and deployed on neuromorphic hardware. They report
45.2\% suppression of alpha--beta oscillations, an 80.0\% reduction in
cumulative stimulation charge against continuous DBS, 0.52\,mW inference
power, and a 28.1$\times$ reduction in energy per inference against an
equivalent artificial neural network on an embedded GPU.


\subsection*{Deciding which contact}

Two works optimise the electrode contact rather than the waveform, in the
context of ventral capsule/ventral striatum stimulation for psychiatric
indications. Nagrale et al.~\cite{nagrale2023bayesian} formulate contact
selection as a multi-armed bandit, each contact an arm with an unknown
behavioural reward distribution, driven by UCB1 over reaction times on the
Multi-Source Interference Task, with amplitude, pulse width and frequency held
fixed. Their simulation environment is fitted to recorded data from six
participants.

Nagrale and Widge~\cite{nagrale2026arena} make three changes to that design
and report the effect of each. Removing the intermediate state-space sensor
model and passing raw reaction times to the bandit lowers the contact ranking
failure rate from 31.9\% to 11.9\%, and avoids a state variance inflation of
51.7\% at baseline and 148.0\% in conflict states. Replacing discrete daily
resets with a continuous trial history reduces mean trial-level regret over
4{,}200 trials. A rolling arena that keeps four of eight contacts active,
benches the weakest and rolls in an untested contact reaches accuracy
comparable to full eight-contact search. They benchmark UCB1, a variance-aware
UCB, Block-Variance Bayes-UCB and Thompson Sampling variants against greedy,
$\varepsilon$-greedy and brute-force search, and list joint optimisation of
amplitude, pulse width and frequency as future work.


\subsection*{Tuning a controller rather than the stimulus}

Mai et al.~\cite{mai2026personalized} retain an explicit controller and use
learning to set its parameters. An unknown-input observer is paired with
nonlinear state-error feedback, and a bounded soft actor--critic policy adjusts
two observer gains and two feedback gains every 20\,ms, giving a
four-dimensional search space; at deployment the policy outputs deterministic
gains within prescribed safety bounds. The reward balances tracking,
smoothness and energy efficiency, and the policy is trained under randomised
coupling and disturbance multipliers. On a delayed subthalamic--globus pallidus
externa firing-rate model whose dominant oscillation lies near 20\,Hz, they
report a mean RMSE of 0.4143 spikes/s against 0.4816 for conventional active
disturbance rejection control and 1.5000 for sliding mode control.


\subsection*{Suppressing synchrony in oscillator ensembles}

Krylov et al.~\cite{krylov2020framework} evaluate on ensembles of abstract
oscillators rather than a biophysical circuit, with synchrony as the
controlled quantity. Their environment spans Bonhoeffer--van der Pol,
Hindmarsh--Rose and Rulkov neuron models, and they report suppression across
three signalling regimes using multiple PPO agents on ensembles of $N = 1000$
neurons. Their reward is
$R_t = -(X(t) - \langle X \rangle_t)^2 - \beta |A(t)|$ with $\beta = 2$,
penalising both residual synchrony and the amplitude of the applied action.
They state that the action space can be constrained so that an agent's actions
remain harmless to the patient, and present the work as an RL environment for
comparing agents rather than as a controller.
