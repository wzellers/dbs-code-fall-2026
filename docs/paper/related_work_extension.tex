\subsection*{Deciding when to stimulate}

The largest group of recent work leaves frequency and amplitude fixed and
learns only whether to deliver stimulation at each step. Mehregan et
al.~\cite{mehregan2024enhancing} discretise the state and action spaces of an
RL controller, rewarding reduced beta-band power while holding stimulation
frequency low, and add post-training and quantisation-aware training to cut
inference cost; they validate in a rodent model as well as in simulation,
which is the only in-vivo evidence among the ten. Ravivarapu et
al.~\cite{ravivarapu2025sea} continue that line directly, adopting the same
group's network model but targeting sample efficiency rather than model size:
a predictive reward model estimates outcomes so the policy need not query the
environment at every update, with Gumbel-Softmax sampling for exploration in
the binary action space, and FP16 quantisation reducing the model from
65\,MB to 33\,MB. Their reward is quadratic in the deviation of mean beta power
from a threshold, and their reported baseline is DDPG.

The two divide the same problem between them --- one compresses a trained
policy, the other reduces the interaction needed to train it --- and both
differ from parameter-tuning approaches in what is left fixed. Stimulation
cost enters neither objective explicitly; in~\cite{ravivarapu2025sea} it
appears only through the sparsity of the chosen pulses.

Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024tremor} also control stimulation delivery, but
against a different symptom and a different model: neurons are represented in
the Izhikevich formalism, the target is desynchronisation in the globus
pallidus internus with tremor attenuation as the outcome, and the authors
report an analysis of the resulting stimulation frequency and a balance
between efficacy and energy consumption, compared against a standard DBS
model. It is the only work among the ten to take tremor rather than a band
power or a synchrony measure as its endpoint.


\subsection*{Deciding the stimulation parameters}

Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2026neuromorphic} tune stimulation parameters
directly rather than pulse delivery. A deep spiking Q-network reads a
biophysical cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic model and emits nine discrete
actions, one of $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ for each of frequency, pulse width and
amplitude, within clinical bounds; the reward combines alpha--beta suppression
with the physical charge delivered. The policy is compressed by
sparsity-constrained knowledge distillation and deployed on neuromorphic
hardware, where the authors report 45.2\% suppression, an 80.0\% reduction in
cumulative stimulation charge against continuous DBS, 0.52\,mW inference
power, and a 28.1$\times$ reduction in energy per inference against an
equivalent conventional network on an embedded GPU.

Two things separate it from the group above. Its action space is relative
rather than absolute --- each step nudges a parameter up or down instead of
selecting a setting --- so the policy encodes a trajectory through the
parameter space rather than a choice within it. And stimulation energy enters
the objective as an explicit term, which among the ten is otherwise true only
of the controller-tuning work below.


\subsection*{Deciding which contact}

A separate line treats the electrode contact, not the waveform, as the
quantity to optimise, and comes from stimulation of the ventral
capsule/ventral striatum for psychiatric indications rather than from
Parkinson's disease. Nagrale et al.~\cite{nagrale2023bayesian} cast contact
selection as a multi-armed bandit, each contact an arm with an unknown
behavioural reward distribution, and drive it with UCB1 using reaction time on
the Multi-Source Interference Task as the signal, holding amplitude, pulse
width and frequency fixed. Their simulation environment is fitted to recorded
data from six participants.

Nagrale and Widge~\cite{nagrale2026arena} revise that design in three ways and
report what each is worth. They remove the intermediate state-space sensor
model and feed raw reaction times to the bandit, which lowers the contact
ranking failure rate from 31.9\% to 11.9\% and avoids a variance inflation of
51.7\% at baseline and 148.0\% in conflict states. They replace discrete daily
resets with one continuous history, which drives mean trial-level regret down
steadily over 4{,}200 trials. And they introduce a rolling arena that keeps
four of eight contacts active, benches the weakest and rolls in an untested
one, reporting that this scales to the full array without sacrificing
accuracy. The comparison spans UCB1, a variance-aware UCB, Block-Variance
Bayes-UCB and Thompson Sampling variants against greedy, $\varepsilon$-greedy
and brute-force search.

The pair is the clearest case in this set of a method correcting itself: the
later paper's main finding is that an estimator placed between the measurement
and the learner cost more in added variance than it recovered. Contact
selection and parameter tuning also remain complementary rather than competing
problems, one choosing where to stimulate and the other what to deliver,
and~\cite{nagrale2026arena} lists joint optimisation of amplitude, pulse width
and frequency as future work.


\subsection*{Tuning a controller rather than the stimulus}

Two works keep an explicit controller and use learning only to set its
parameters. In the PPO-tuned fractional-order PID
framework~\cite{ppofopid2025}, a proximal policy optimisation agent tunes all
five parameters of a FOPID controller --- the three gains $K_p$, $K_i$ and
$K_d$ together with the fractional orders $\lambda$ and $\mu$ --- inside a
macroscopic neurophysiological model, with a machine-learning diagnostic
module upstream; evaluated against open-loop and conventional PID on RMS
tremor error and stimulation energy, the authors report a steady-state RMS
error of 0.0604 and a 44.38\% improvement over open-loop control. Mai et
al.~\cite{mai2026personalized} pair an unknown-input observer with nonlinear
state-error feedback and use a bounded soft actor--critic policy to adjust two
observer gains and two feedback gains every 20\,ms, on a delayed
subthalamic--globus pallidus externa firing-rate model whose dominant
oscillation sits near 20\,Hz; against conventional active disturbance
rejection control and sliding mode control they report mean RMSE of 0.4143
spikes/s versus 0.4816 and 1.5000.

What both learn is how to regulate, not what to deliver: their action spaces
are controller gains, so the stimulus is whatever the tuned controller
produces. This is also the only group here that addresses stability
directly --- \cite{ppofopid2025} through fractional-order control and
\cite{mai2026personalized} through a disturbance-rejection structure with
bounded gain actions. Their reported outcomes, RMS tremor error and
firing-rate RMSE, are not commensurable with band-power measures, so the
percentages are improvements over different baselines on different
quantities.


\subsection*{Suppressing synchrony in oscillator ensembles}

The last group evaluates on ensembles of abstract oscillators rather than
biophysical circuits, and takes synchrony itself as the pathology. Krylov et
al.~\cite{krylov2020framework} released an RL environment spanning
Bonhoeffer--van der Pol, Hindmarsh--Rose and Rulkov neuron models,
demonstrated suppression across three signalling regimes with multiple PPO
agents, and noted that the action space can be constrained so that an agent's
actions remain harmless. BGRL~\cite{bgrl2024} works in the same setting and on
the same three regimes --- regular, chaotic and bursting --- but structures the
agent after the region being treated, drawing on the resemblance between the
basal ganglia and an actor--critic architecture; against a soft actor--critic
baseline the authors report improvements of 40\%, 146\% and 40\% across the
three regimes, and argue the approach also reduces energy consumption.

The two differ in where the biology is placed. Elsewhere in this section a
biophysical model serves as the environment and is paired with a generic
learner; BGRL instead takes a simplified environment and draws its
architectural inspiration into the agent. The two choices are orthogonal.
This group also differs from all the others in its endpoint: synchrony
measured across an ensemble, rather than a band power, a behavioural response,
or a tremor amplitude.
